MPPT Solar Charge Controller

Introduction

Solar photovoltaic (PV) systems exhibit nonlinear voltage- current characteristics, and their maximum power point (MPP) varies continuously with changes in solar irradiance and temperature. Conventional charge controllers operate the solar panel at a fixed operating point, which leads to significant power loss and reduced charging efficiency.

Additionally, improper charging of batteries can cause overcharging, deep discharging, heating, and reduced battery lifespan. Hence, there is a need for a smart solar charge controller that can:

  • Continuously extract maximum available power from the solar panel.
  • Efficiently regulate the charging process of the battery.
  • Ensure safe, reliable, and optimal charging under varying environmental conditions.

in this project, I designed and implemented an MPPT-based solar charge controller using a buck converter, controlled by an embedded system, to maximize energy harvesting and ensure efficient battery charging

Maximum Power Point

Photovoltaic (PV) cells exhibit a nonlinear voltage-current (V-I) characteristic, where the current remains nearly constant at low voltages and then drops sharply as the voltage approaches the open-circuit value (Voc). At zero voltage, the current is maximum, known as the short-circuit current (Isc), while at zero current, the voltage reaches Voc. The power output of the PV cell, given by P = V × I, varies with the operating point and produces a power-voltage (P-V) curve that peaks at the Maximum Power Point (MPP). This point, defined by Vmpp and Impp, represents the condition where the panel delivers maximum power. Since the MPP shifts with changes in irradiance and temperature, MPPT techniques are required to ensure optimal energy extraction.

Perturb and Observe Algorithm

The Perturb and Observe (P&O) method is used to track the maximum power point. First, the solar panel voltage (V) and current (I) are measured, and the output power, P = V × I, is calculated. Then, the present power value is compared with the previous power value. If power increases, the duty cycle continues changing in the same direction; if power decreases, the duty cycle direction is reversed. PWM signals based on the updated duty cycle are used to drive the converter. In this project, the buck converter is driven at a frequency of 19 kHz with a 12-bit resolution. This is looped continuously to always maintain operation at the maximum power point.

Li-ion Charging

A typical lithium-ion cell has a nominal voltage of 3.6 V with a maximum voltage of 4.2 V and is generally discharged to a cutoff voltage of 2.5 V. Charging is performed using a constant current–constant voltage (CC–CV) profile. In the initial stage, the system operates in constant current mode, where a fixed current is supplied to the battery, causing its voltage to rise gradually. Once the battery voltage reaches a predefined threshold (4.2 V for Li-ion cells), the system transitions to constant voltage mode, where the voltage is kept constant and the charging current decreases as the battery approaches full charge. This method makes sure the battery is charging efficiently while preventing overcurrent and overvoltage, thereby improving battery life and safety.

System Overview

The control algorithm operates in multiple modes to ensure efficiency and safe battery charging. Initially, an Under-Voltage Lockout (UVLO) condition verifies whether the input voltage is sufficient for normal operation; if insufficient, the converter is disabled. Once active, the system operates across MPPT, Constant Current (CC), or Constant Voltage (CV) modes. In MPPT mode, the Perturb and Observe (P&O) algorithm continuously tracks the Maximum Power Point by adjusting the PWM duty cycle based on real-time changes in input power and voltage. An outer feedback loop continuously monitors the output: if the current exceeds the safe threshold, the controller regulates via CC mode, and once the battery voltage reaches its limit, it transitions into CV mode.

The entire working logic is driven by an ESP32 microcontroller. As the solar panel generates a DC voltage dependent on irradiance, an INA219 sensor measures panel voltage and current over an I2C bus. The microcontroller computes the instantaneous power (P = V × I) and dynamically adjusts the duty cycle to generate a high-frequency PWM driving signal for the buck converter switches. The synchronous buck converter steps down the voltage to supply the battery while continuous monitoring prevents overcharging, maintaining safe and optimal charging throughput.
